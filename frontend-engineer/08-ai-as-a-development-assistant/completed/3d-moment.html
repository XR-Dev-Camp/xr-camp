Checking an AI's explanation of a 3D scene

Below is a small three.js scene, and an "AI explanation" of its code. Ana checked every claim against the official three.js documentation. Three of the seven claims were wrong. Then she asked her own assistant about the same code, and checked its answer the same way.

A purple torus knot, a tube tied in a knot like a pretzel, floats in the middle of a pale background. Light comes from the upper right, so that side is brighter. It turns slowly around its vertical axis.

The code

const camera = new THREE.PerspectiveCamera(50, 1, 0.1, 100);
scene.add(new THREE.AmbientLight('#ffffff', 0.6));

const sun = new THREE.DirectionalLight('#ffffff', 2.5);
sun.position.set(3, 4, 5);

const knot = new THREE.Mesh(
  new THREE.TorusKnotGeometry(1, 0.32, 128, 16),
  new THREE.MeshStandardMaterial({ color: '#5b2a86', roughness: 0.35 }),
);

const TURN_SPEED = THREE.MathUtils.degToRad(30);   // per second
knot.rotation.y += TURN_SPEED * seconds;

renderer.setAnimationLoop(paused ? null : frame);

The AI's explanation, checked

A made-up example. XR Camp wrote this "AI explanation" for practice, with mistakes on purpose, the kind real assistants make. It is not the output of any real product.

  1. "PerspectiveCamera(50, 1, 0.1, 100): 50 is the vertical field of view, in degrees. 0.1 and 100 are the nearest and farthest distances the camera draws."

    Correct The docs say fov is "the vertical field of view, from bottom to top of view, in degrees", and the last two are the near and far planes. Source: three.js docs: PerspectiveCamera.

  2. "AmbientLight lights every object equally, from no particular direction, so it cannot cast shadows."

    Correct The docs: it "globally illuminates all objects in the scene equally", and cannot cast shadows "as it does not have a direction". Source: three.js docs: AmbientLight.

  3. "DirectionalLight works like a light bulb at (3, 4, 5): its light spreads out in all directions from there, so objects closer to it are brighter."

    Wrong A directional light behaves "as though it is infinitely far away", and its rays "are all parallel", pointing from its position to its target (the centre of the scene, unless you move it). Distance makes no difference. The light bulb is a different class, PointLight. Sources: three.js docs: DirectionalLight, PointLight.

  4. "TorusKnotGeometry(1, 0.32, 128, 16): 1 is the radius of the knot, 0.32 the radius of the tube. 128 and 16 are numbers of segments: more makes it smoother, and more work to draw."

    Correct Radius, tube, tubular segments, and radial segments, in that order. The two numbers left out, p and q, keep their defaults (2 and 3), which set how the knot winds. Source: three.js docs: TorusKnotGeometry.

  5. "MeshStandardMaterial needs light to be seen. To make the scene cheaper, switch to MeshBasicMaterial: it is shaded by the same lights, but faster."

    Wrong The docs say MeshBasicMaterial "is not affected by lights". Ana tried it: the knot became one flat purple shape, with no bright side and no shadow side, so you can no longer see that it is a knot. Sources: three.js docs: MeshBasicMaterial, MeshStandardMaterial.

  6. "You do not need degToRad. Rotations in three.js are in degrees, like A-Frame's rotation attribute, so knot.rotation.y += 30 * seconds does the same thing."

    Wrong rotation is "the object's local rotation as Euler angles, in radians". A-Frame's rotation attribute is in degrees, but A-Frame converts it for three.js. Thirty radians a second is almost five turns a second: a fast spin that could make someone feel unwell. degToRad(30) is the right way to say "30 degrees". Sources: three.js docs: Object3D, MathUtils, A-Frame docs: rotation.

  7. "Passing null to setAnimationLoop stops the loop. three.js recommends setAnimationLoop instead of calling requestAnimationFrame yourself."

    Correct Both are in the docs for setAnimationLoop. That is how the Pause button on this page works. Source: three.js docs: WebGLRenderer.

What Ana learned

The wrong claims sounded just as confident as the right ones. Two of them mixed up things that are true somewhere else: a point light does spread out like a bulb, and A-Frame does use degrees. Checking took about twenty minutes, and the docs answered every question.
