Case study: Riverside Community Centre website

How I planned, built, tested, and improved a website that works on any screen and for people who use assistive technology.

My role
Everything, on my own: planning, writing, design, code, and testing.
Time
About 70 hours, over six XR Camp courses.
Tools
HTML, CSS, JavaScript, VS Code, browser developer tools, NVDA, VoiceOver, and axe DevTools.
Honest note
Riverside is an imagined centre. This was a learning project, so there are no real visitors yet.
Links
Visit the Riverside website

The problem

A small community centre needs one place where people can find three things quickly: when it is open, what activities it runs, and how to get there. Most of its visitors check on a phone, often on a slow connection, and some use a screen reader or large text.

A site full of pictures and animation would look impressive, but it would be slow and hard to use for exactly the people the centre serves. The goal was the opposite: fast, clear, and usable by everyone.

Who it is for

  • Parents looking for the homework club or holiday activities, usually on a phone.
  • Adults learning English, who need short sentences and plain words.
  • Older neighbours, who often make the text bigger.
  • Blind and low-vision visitors, who use a screen reader or zoom.

My process

I built the site in six stages, and each stage added one layer to the one before.

  1. Content and structure. I wrote the words first, then marked them up with headings, landmarks, a table, and a captioned video. The HTML worked before it had any style.
  2. A form. I added a page for joining a programme, with a visible label on every field and error messages that say how to fix the problem.
  3. A visual system. I wrote one stylesheet with named colours, sizes, and spaces, and checked every text colour for contrast.
  4. Responsive layout. I rebuilt the layout mobile first, then tested at four widths and on real phones.
  5. An accessibility audit. I audited a page with the keyboard, a screen reader, zoom, and an automated tool, then applied the same checks to the whole site.
  6. Interaction. I added a programme explorer, where visitors can search, filter, and save programmes.

Decisions I made, and why

1. Mobile first

What I chose: I wrote the styles for a 320-pixel phone first, then added layout for bigger screens.

Why: most visitors use phones, so the phone page must be the best one, not a squeezed version of the desktop page.

What I considered: designing for a wide screen first. I tried it on one page, and needed many rules just to undo things on small screens.

2. Programmes as a list of cards

What I chose: a normal HTML list, laid out as a grid of cards with one line of CSS:

grid-template-columns: repeat(auto-fill, minmax(min(100%, 15rem), 1fr));

Why: a screen reader still announces “list, 6 items”, and the grid makes one column on a phone and three on a wide screen with no media query at all.

3. A table that scrolls inside its own box

What I chose: the opening-hours table stays a real table, inside a box that scrolls sideways on very narrow screens and can be reached with the keyboard.

Why: a real table lets screen-reader users hear the day with every time.

What I considered: changing the table into blocks with CSS. It looked fine, but changing how a table displays can make some screen readers stop treating it as a table.

4. Announce results instead of moving focus

What I chose: in the programme explorer, the number of results is in an element with role="status", so it is announced while the visitor keeps typing.

Why: my first version moved focus to the results after every key. With a screen reader on, I could not finish typing a word.

Accessibility

I aimed for WCAG 2.2 level AA. Some of the work is invisible, so I list it here:

  • A skip link, one h1 per page, and headings in order.
  • Alt text that says what matters, such as the step-free ramp, and empty alt for decoration.
  • Every text colour at 4.5:1 contrast or more, and a focus outline you can always see.
  • Links and buttons at least 44 pixels tall, so they are easy to tap.
  • Captions on the welcome video.
  • Nothing on the site moves on its own.

How I tested

Tests on the finished site
Test What I found What I changed
Keyboard only The opening-hours box could not be reached, so it could not be scrolled. Added tabindex="0" and a label to the box.
Screen reader (NVDA, VoiceOver) Two links both said “Find out more”. Rewrote them to say where they go.
200% zoom and 320 pixels wide The navigation ran off the screen. Let it wrap onto a second line with flexbox.
Automated check (axe DevTools) One grey text colour was 3.9:1. Darkened it to 8.5:1.
Two real phones The join button was hard to tap next to a link. Made it taller and added space between them.

What is not perfect yet

  • The welcome video has English captions only.
  • There is no Spanish version of the site yet, although many neighbours would prefer one.

Results

Because Riverside is imagined, I cannot measure real visitors. What I can measure:

  • The automated check reports no errors on any page.
  • No page scrolls sideways at 320 pixels wide.
  • On a phone, the home page picture is 6 KB instead of 24 KB, because the phone downloads the smallest size.
  • Every task, from finding the opening hours to saving a programme, works with the keyboard alone.

What I learned

  • Accessibility is easier from the start. Fixing the events page took hours; building the explorer accessibly from the first line took minutes more.
  • Automated tools find only some problems. The duplicate “Find out more” links passed the automated check. I found them by listening.
  • Plain words are harder than long ones. I rewrote the About section four times to make it shorter.

What I would do next

Add a Spanish version, with captions in both languages, and test the site with two or three real neighbours instead of only with tools.
