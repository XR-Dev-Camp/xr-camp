Case study: the virtual cultural exhibit

A 3D exhibit that also works as a WebXR experience, and still works with no headset at all.

The problem

Most 3D "virtual museum" demos I found online assume a fast connection, a powerful GPU, and a mouse or a headset. That leaves out exactly the learners XR Camp is built for. I wanted to build a small cultural exhibit that a visitor could explore with a keyboard alone, that stayed readable for someone using a screen reader, and that still loaded on a modest laptop.

My role

I built this alone, across several XR Camp phases, as the ongoing project each lesson added to. I was the developer, the accessibility reviewer, and the person writing the on-page text for every exhibit.

Approach

  • Phase 3 (Web3D Developer): built the exhibits in three.js with GLTFLoader, keyboard-accessible raycasting for selection, and on-page attribution for every CC0 model.
  • Performance: measured before optimising, with renderer.info and Chrome's Performance panel, then applied instancing, level of detail, and lazy loading of exhibits the visitor has not reached yet.
  • Phase 4 (Immersive Developer): added an optional WebXR mode (controllers, hand tracking where supported, and an AR placement mode), with a seated option and a comfort vignette during locomotion.
  • Accessibility throughout: a text scene description next to every 3D view, a 2D list of every exhibit's information, keyboard routes for each interaction, and a working non-XR fallback, checked against the W3C's XR Accessibility User Requirements (XAUR).

Result

A capstone exhibit with several rooms, a working keyboard route through all of them, a scene description a screen reader can read on its own, and a WebXR mode that degrades gracefully on a device with no headset or hand tracking. On my own machine, applying instancing and level of detail cut the exhibit's draw calls by more than half; exact numbers are in the Phase 3 performance-engineering lesson, stated as "on my machine" rather than a general claim.

What I would do differently

I under-estimated how much text a scene description needs before it is actually useful, not just present. Writing one late, after a room's geometry was finished, meant rewriting it twice. Next time I will write the scene description first, as a short 2D outline, and build the 3D room to match it.
