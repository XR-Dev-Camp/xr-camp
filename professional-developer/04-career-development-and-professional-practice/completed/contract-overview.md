# Overview of contracts

> **This is not legal advice, and laws differ by country.** This document explains what a freelance or contract-work agreement commonly covers, in plain language, so you know what to look for and what to ask about. Before signing a real contract, or before relying on one you wrote, get it reviewed by a qualified person in your own country and the client's, if the work or the money involved is significant to you.

A written agreement protects both sides by making expectations explicit before a disagreement happens, not after. Four areas come up in almost every freelance contract:

## Scope

What work is actually included, and, just as importantly, what is not. A scope section usually lists specific deliverables (see "A simple proposal" in [`freelancing-basics.md`](freelancing-basics.md)) and states how a change to that list is handled — commonly, that new work outside the agreed scope needs a separate written agreement or additional payment, so the project cannot quietly grow without anyone agreeing to it.

## Payment terms

This covers the price, the currency, the schedule (for example, a deposit before work starts, a milestone payment partway through, and a final payment on delivery), and what happens if a payment is late. Many freelancers ask for a portion of the payment upfront specifically because it confirms the client is serious and shares some of the risk of the work starting.

## Intellectual property (IP)

This decides who owns the finished work once it is paid for, and often, separately, who owns the individual pieces used to build it. A common pattern is that full ownership of the final deliverable transfers to the client on final payment, while the freelancer keeps the right to show the work in a portfolio unless the client specifically asks for confidentiality. Confidentiality (sometimes called an NDA, a non-disclosure agreement) is a related but separate promise not to share the client's private information; it does not by itself decide who owns anything.

## Licensing of assets

Separate from who owns the final deliverable, a contract should say what a freelancer is allowed to reuse from the project afterwards, and what license covers any third-party assets (fonts, images, 3D models, code libraries) used inside it. If you use a CC0 or CC-BY asset in a client project, the way this course has throughout, the client needs to know that licence's terms too, since they are the one running the finished site afterwards, the same way this repository's own [`ATTRIBUTION.md`](../ATTRIBUTION.md) files exist so nobody loses track of where an asset came from or what it can be used for.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | What happens | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting work on a verbal agreement only | Either side may remember the deal differently once money is involved | Get the scope and price in writing, even briefly, before starting |
| A scope so vague it could mean anything ("build me a website") | Constant renegotiation over what counts as "done" | List specific, checkable deliverables |
| No mention of what happens if a payment is late | An unpaid invoice has no agreed next step | State a payment schedule and what happens if it slips |
| Assuming "I built it, so I own it" or the reverse, with nothing written down | A dispute over ownership has no written answer to point to | Say explicitly who owns the final work, and from what point |

## Where to learn more

Contract law is set at the country (and sometimes regional) level, so a single lesson cannot tell you what is enforceable where you live or where your client lives. If a project is small and low-risk, a short, clear written agreement covering the four areas above is far better than nothing. If it is not, a qualified local professional, or a free or low-cost legal-aid service where one exists in your country, is worth the time.
