# Freelancing basics

This document is a starting map, not a complete business course. It covers four things every new freelancer needs on day one: finding clients, writing a proposal, estimating honestly, and sending an invoice. No fees, rates, or "typical market price" figures appear anywhere in it; prices vary enormously by country, currency, and specialism, and a number printed here would go stale and mislead someone relying on it.

## Finding clients

- **Start with people who already trust your work.** A finished portfolio project, a XR Camp classmate, or a local community organisation you already helped is a warmer lead than a stranger on a job board.
- **A public case study does real work here.** The one in [`case-study.html`](case-study.html) is written so a stranger can judge your process, not just your finished screenshot.
- **Job boards and freelance platforms are one channel, not the only one.** Read a platform's fee structure and dispute process before relying on it; policies and availability change, and some platforms are not reachable, or work differently, from every country.
- **Local and community networks matter.** A small NGO, school, or local business may need a simple accessible website and never think to look on an international platform at all.
- **Be specific about what you offer.** "I build accessible websites and small 3D exhibits, and I test everything by keyboard" tells a potential client more than "I do web development."

## Remote work across time zones

Most freelance web and XR work is remote by default. A few habits make that workable instead of stressful:

- **State your time zone and working hours plainly**, in your CV and in your first message to a client, using a fixed reference like UTC rather than only your local name for it (which the client may not recognise).
- **Prefer written updates to synchronous calls** where possible. A short written status update a client can read on their own schedule respects a large time-zone gap far better than insisting on a live call at an inconvenient hour for one side.
- **Agree meeting times in one written time**, and let each side convert it locally, rather than trusting a verbal "let's say 3pm" that silently assumes whose 3pm it is.
- **Working with clients in Latin America and in mainland China is completely normal for a remote developer**, and each region has its own common working hours, holidays, and communication norms (for example, some regions favour more written coordination and less real-time chat than others). Ask directly, early, rather than assuming your own habits are the default.

## A simple proposal

A proposal answers four questions: what you will do, when, for how much, and what happens if the scope changes. Keep it short enough that a busy client reads all of it.

```markdown
# Proposal: [project name]

Prepared for: [client name]
Prepared by: [your name]
Date: [date]

## What I understand you need
[1-2 sentences restating the client's request in your own words, so a
misunderstanding surfaces now, not after the work starts.]

## What I will deliver
- [Specific deliverable 1, e.g. "a 5-page accessible website"]
- [Specific deliverable 2]
- What is explicitly *not* included (this prevents "scope creep" later)

## Timeline
[Start date] to [end date], with a check-in on [date].

## Price
[A range, or a fixed price — see "Estimating with ranges and a buffer" below]
Payment terms: [e.g. "50% to start, 50% on delivery" — see the contract
overview for more on payment terms]

## What happens if the scope changes
If you'd like something beyond this list, I am glad to quote it separately
rather than absorbing it into this price silently.
```

## Estimating with ranges and a buffer

A single fixed number invites two problems: it is usually wrong, and a client reasonably expects you to hit it exactly.

1. **Break the work into pieces you have actually done before.** "Build five accessible pages," "wire up a contact form," "write the case study."
2. **Estimate each piece in hours, as a range**, not a single number: "6-9 hours," not "7 hours." The range itself communicates your uncertainty honestly.
3. **Add a buffer of roughly 15-25% on top of your total**, for the things every project has: a slow client reply, a browser bug, a piece of content that arrives late. Say so plainly if a client asks why the buffer exists; it is normal practice, not padding.
4. **Convert to a price range only once you have a rate you are comfortable naming**, in a currency your client will recognise, and state which currency clearly (this document deliberately does not suggest a rate).

| Task | Estimated hours (range) |
| --- | --- |
| Plan and wireframe | 2-3 |
| Build pages | 6-9 |
| Accessibility pass and testing | 2-3 |
| Buffer (~20%) | 2-3 |
| **Total** | **12-18** |

## Invoicing basics

An invoice is a request for payment with enough detail that both sides can check it later. At minimum, include:

- Your name (or business name) and contact details
- The client's name
- An invoice number and date (numbering them in order, e.g. `2026-001`, `2026-002`, makes your own records easier to check)
- A short description of the work and the period it covers
- The amount, the currency, and the due date
- How to pay (see "Payment options" below)

Keep a copy of every invoice you send, even a simple one, and note the date it was paid. This record is what a tax authority, or a future you trying to remember what a client agreed to, will actually need.

### Payment options, named generally

Do not assume the payment method you are used to works for a client in another country, or that theirs works for you. Before agreeing a price, check, for your specific pair of countries and currencies:

- **Bank transfer** (domestic or international wire): usually reliable, but international transfers can carry a fee on one or both ends, and can take several business days.
- **A payment platform or processor** that operates in both your country and your client's: availability, fees, and verification requirements vary by country and change over time, so check current terms directly with the provider rather than assuming.
- **A local payment app or e-wallet** common in the client's country: useful for a client based in that country, but check that you can actually receive funds into it, and convert them, from where you are.

For clients in Latin America or mainland China specifically, agree the payment method and currency in writing before starting work, and ask the client which options are normal and reliable on their end; the honest answer is that this varies by country, by bank, and by year, so a name printed in a lesson would go out of date.
